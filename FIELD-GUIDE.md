# Field Guide: El Salvador Archaeology, Ethnohistory, and Legend

This is a broad orientation to the corpus, not a settled narrative. It is
designed to reveal the range of promising subjects before deep research begins.

## The strongest organizing idea

El Salvador is small in modern area but sits across several overlapping worlds:
the southeastern Maya frontier, Pacific coastal exchange, Nahua migration and
state formation, Lenca and Cacaopera/Kakawira eastern traditions, the Gulf of
Fonseca, and a volcanic landscape that repeatedly destroyed and preserved
settlements. The most productive research questions usually cross at least two
of those frames.

## High-value archaeological clusters

### Chalchuapa and the western highlands

Chalchuapa is not one site but a long-lived archaeological zone. Treat these as
linked but distinct subjects:

- **El Trapiche** — early monumental construction and Preclassic occupation.
- **Casa Blanca** — Preclassic/Classic ceremonial architecture and modern
  archaeological park.
- **Tazumal** — the best-known monumental complex; excavation history includes
  heavy reconstruction and changing interpretations.
- **Laguna Cuzcachapa** — a settlement and ritual landscape within the larger
  Chalchuapa zone.
- **Finca Rosita, Nuevo Tazumal, and Los Gavilanes** — less famous components
  that complicate a monument-centered view of Chalchuapa.
- **Cerro El Trapiche/El Trapiche mound group chronology** — a major anchor for
  regional ceramic and architectural sequences.

Start with the local FUNDAR copies of [Coe's El Trapiche excavations](sources/fundar/coe_trapiche.pdf),
[Boggs on Tazumal](sources/fundar/tzunpame_boggs_tazumal.pdf), and
[Erquicia on Los Gavilanes](sources/institutional/2007-erquicia-los-gavilanes.pdf).

### Cara Sucia and the Cotzumalhuapa connection

Cara Sucia is the clearest Salvadoran route into the Cotzumalhuapa cultural
sphere centered on Guatemala's Pacific piedmont. It deserves focused comparison
of sculpture, ballgame imagery, exchange, and the political meaning of a
cross-border archaeological style. This is exactly the kind of neighboring-
country connection that belongs here because the Salvadoran material cannot be
understood in isolation.

### Valle de Zapotitán

The valley brings together volcanology, household archaeology, political
centers, agriculture, and colonial reuse:

- **Joya de Cerén** — exceptional household, agricultural, and organic
  preservation beneath the Loma Caldera eruption.
- **San Andrés** — a Classic-period regional center, later hacienda, and
  archaeologically documented colonial indigo works.
- **El Cambio** — a major Preclassic/Classic site heavily affected by modern
  development.
- **Chanmico, El Chahüite, Nuevo Lourdes Poniente, Chuchucato, and other valley
  sites** — important for settlement patterns beyond the parks.
- **Ilopango Tierra Blanca Joven (TBJ)** — chronology and human response remain
  active research subjects; older dates and catastrophic narratives must be
  checked against newer volcanological work.

Useful local starting points include [Dimick's 1941 San Andrés notes](sources/fundar/dimick_1941.pdf),
[Boggs's San Andrés report](sources/fundar/tzunpame_boggs_sanandres.pdf),
[Amaroli and Dull on preserved milpas](sources/fundar/milpas.pdf), and the
[UNESCO/ICOMOS evaluation of Joya de Cerén](https://whc.unesco.org/document/154008).

### Cihuatán, Las Marías, and the Early Postclassic

Cihuatán is one of the richest research clusters in the archive. Topics include
urban extent, burned architecture, household organization, archaeobotany,
figurines, Mexican-style deities and iconography, the circular temple, defensive
features, and the city's violent end. Las Marías is closely related but should
not be reduced to an appendage of Cihuatán.

Older “Toltec colony” or direct central-Mexican migration models are historically
important but require reassessment. Similar objects or iconography do not by
themselves establish ethnicity, conquest, or political control.

The FUNDAR collection contains the primary research sequence, including the
[1977–78 monograph](sources/fundar/bruhns.pdf), [P-7 excavation report](sources/fundar/P7.pdf),
[burned palace notice](<sources/fundar/Palacio 2004.pdf>), [site-boundary survey](sources/fundar/limits.pdf),
[archaeobotanical final report](sources/fundar/botanical_final.pdf), and
[Las Marías Tláloc rescue report](sources/fundar/tlalocreport.pdf).

### Lake Güija and underwater/island archaeology

Lake Güija links El Salvador and Guatemala and combines settlement, sculpture,
rock art, islands, inundated contexts, and modern heritage threats.

- **Igualtepeque** — a Postclassic island/peninsula site and major public-
  archaeology case.
- **Isla Teopán** — source of a potbelly sculpture that prompted reinterpretation
  of the genre.
- **Lake Güija plaque** — an inscribed object relevant to Maya writing and
  cross-regional contact.
- **Güija Xipe Tótec sculptures** — artifact histories, looting/provenance, and
  Nahua religious interpretation.

See the [Igualtepeque threat report](sources/fundar/igualtepeque.pdf),
[Lake Güija plaque study](sources/fundar/guijaplaque.pdf), [Teopán sculpture study](sources/fundar/teopan.pdf),
and [Boggs on the Güija Xipe sculptures](sources/fundar/boggs_xipes.pdf).

### Eastern El Salvador and the Gulf of Fonseca

The east is essential precisely because it resists simple “Maya versus Pipil”
classification:

- **Quelepa** — long sequence, monumental center, eastern interaction networks,
  and a key test case for assigning archaeological remains to historical Lenca
  identity.
- **Asanyamba** — Gulf of Fonseca ceramic assemblage and maritime connections.
- **Gruta/Cueva del Espíritu Santo at Corinto** — the country's largest known
  concentration of rock paintings, with uncertain chronology and affiliation.
- **Morazán non-ceramic complex** — an important but interpretation-sensitive
  archaeological category.
- **Guatajiagua pottery** — ethnographic documentation relevant to technological
  continuity and change.
- **Usulután ovens and eastern ceramics** — production and exchange rather than
  just typological labels.

Begin with [Andrews on Quelepa flutes](sources/fundar/wyllys_flautas.pdf),
[Beaudry on Asanyamba ceramics](sources/fundar/beaudry_asanyamba.pdf),
[Haberland on Corinto](sources/fundar/haberland_corinto.pdf),
[Coladan's rock-art synthesis](sources/institutional/1998-coladan-pinturas-rupestres-oriente.pdf),
and [Lothrop on Guatajiagua potters](sources/fundar/lothrop_guatajiagua.pdf).

### Cuscatlán, Tacuzcalco, and conquest landscapes

The location and nature of the Nahua polity conventionally called the Señorío
de Cuzcatlán should be treated as a research problem, not a fixed map. Separate:

- the colonial spellings and meanings of Cuscatlán/Cuzcatlán;
- archaeological settlement in and around Antiguo Cuscatlán;
- the capital described in conquest-era texts;
- later nationalist reconstructions;
- Tacuzcalco/Tacuscalco and Acajutla as resistance and battle landscapes;
- Cinacantan as a fortified refuge and conquest episode;
- the historical geography of Izalco, Nahulingo, Caluco, Nahuizalco, and the
  cacao-producing Pacific towns.

The indispensable local source trail is [Alvarado's 1524 letters](sources/public-domain/documentosantigu00alva.pdf),
[García de Palacio's 1576 report](sources/public-domain/sansalvadorundh00beregoog.pdf),
[Amaroli's *En la búsqueda de Cuscatlán*](sources/fundar/buscacuscatlan.pdf),
[the Antiguo Cuscatlán investigations](sources/institutional/1994-amaroli-hermes-velasquez-antiguo-cuscatlan.pdf),
and Flores Manzano's [2025 synthesis and preservation-zone revisit](sources/markdown/2025-flores-manzano-despues-ilopango/2025-flores-manzano-despues-ilopango.md).
The 2025 paper is especially useful for tracing unpublished 1992–95 rescue
records, but distinguish the earlier excavators' evidence from Flores Manzano's
2024 observations and from his explicitly hypothetical reconstruction.

### Historical archaeology

Do not stop at 1524. Strong Salvadoran projects cover:

- **Ciudad Vieja/La Bermuda** — the early Spanish town of San Salvador and a
  rare archaeological window onto first-generation colonial life.
- **Colonial churches** — Asunción in Ahuachapán and Izalco, Caluco, and other
  ruined or rebuilt sacred spaces.
- **Indigo obrajes** — San Andrés and the much wider network in San Vicente, La
  Paz, San Miguel, and the Torola drainage.
- **Rail, industrial, military, funerary, and republican archaeology** — present
  in newer Salvadoran work and worth indexing separately from pre-Hispanic sites.

The [PAHES first-phase report](sources/institutional/2009-erquicia-sitios-arqueologicos-historicos.pdf),
[San Vicente/La Paz indigo survey](sources/institutional/2013-erquicia-obrajes-anil.pdf),
[Ahuachapán church investigation](sources/fundar/asuncion_ahuachapan.pdf), and
[Izalco church report and photographs](sources/fundar/asuncion_izalco.pdf)
provide a strong entry.

## Peoples, languages, and cultural labels

These identities must not be projected mechanically backward from colonial or
modern labels onto artifacts:

- **Nahua/Pipil/Nawat** — language, migration traditions, archaeology, colonial
  documents, and modern community identity are overlapping but different
  evidentiary domains. “Pipil” itself has contested usage; preserve source terms
  while noting current community preferences.
- **Lenca** — especially relevant to eastern El Salvador and Quelepa, but direct
  ethnic attribution of earlier archaeological phases requires argument.
- **Cacaopera/Kakawira** — northeastern/eastern El Salvador, language loss and
  revival, oral history, and connections toward Matagalpan-speaking regions.
- **Poqomam and Ch'orti' Maya** — important in western/northern border histories
  and regional comparison; include only with specific Salvadoran relevance.
- **Xinca, Chorotega, Ulua, and other southeastern frontier labels** — potential
  comparative or documentary leads, not blanket explanations.

For the history of the “Pipil question,” compare [Amaroli's early Pipil manuscript](sources/fundar/earliest_pipil.pdf),
[Haberland on Marihua red-on-buff](sources/fundar/haberland_marihua.pdf),
[Vivó Escoto on Nahuat settlement](sources/fundar/vivo_escoto_poblamiento.pdf),
and [Bruhns on the southeastern frontier](sources/fundar/frontier.pdf).

## Artifact and technology trails

Each of these can become its own source-linked cluster:

- Xipe Tótec sculptures and their find contexts.
- Tláloc effigies/incensarios, including the Baratta collection and Las Marías.
- Yacatecuhtli identification in Salvadoran material.
- Potbelly sculptures and the Teopán reassessment.
- Wheeled figurines, especially Cihuatán and eastern finds.
- Pre-Columbian flutes, ocarinas, and other wind instruments.
- Pottery cayuco/boat models and Gulf-Pacific maritime interpretations.
- Olmec-style concave mirror from El Salvador.
- Copador, Gualpopa, Campana, Salúa, Plumbate, Marihua, Mazapan-style, and
  Usulután negative ceramic categories.
- Obsidian production and exchange.
- Earthen architecture, molded adobe, wattle-and-daub, and conservation.
- Ancient fields, plant casts, botanical remains, cacao, maize, agave, and
  household storage.

The FUNDAR index is especially valuable here: [downloaded reference catalogue](sources/fundar/INDEX.md).

## Legends, oral traditions, and mythic complexes

The most important rule is to distinguish a documented narrative from claims
about its age or origin. A twentieth-century collector may preserve Indigenous
language testimony, reshape it, combine multiple traditions, or place it inside
a nationalist framework—all at once.

| Lead | What to investigate | Evidence caution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Siguanaba/Sihuanaba | Salvadoran variants, water/road encounters, links to wider Central American traditions | “Pre-Columbian origin” is often asserted more confidently than demonstrated |
| Cipitío | Izalco/Nahua-language narratives, reversed feet, relationship to Siguanaba | Proposed derivations from Xipe Tótec require source criticism |
| Cadejo | White/black dogs, night travel, gendered guardianship, regional variants | Shared widely across Central America; do not label uniquely Salvadoran |
| Cuyancúa | Serpentine/amphibious water-earth being, Izalco and western place traditions | Track earliest attestations and local narrators |
| Chasca | Lake/water maiden traditions and modern literary retellings | Separate oral record from authored nationalist literature |
| Sisimite | Wild person/giant complex extending into Honduras and Guatemala | Cross-border comparison is necessary but should stay tied to Salvadoran testimony |
| Managuas, Tepehuas, Suyanpa/Sihuapilapa | Water, storm, landscape, and female-spirit traditions | Spellings and categories vary by collector |
| Tutecus | Guardian spirits associated with animals or geographic features | Seek direct Nawat testimony rather than encyclopedic synthesis |
| Don Norte/Ehecatl | Wind personification and possible Nahua religious correspondences | Linguistic similarity alone is not continuity proof |
| Nantzin Papalut | Maternal/butterfly-associated figure in modern Pipil mythology summaries | Locate the underlying Schultze-Jena or community-language passage |
| Flor de amate | Supernatural flowering tree and wealth/pact narratives | Often appears in later folklore compilations |
| Carreta chillona | Death cart, war dead, curfew/night narratives | Regional colonial/Christian motifs likely interact with local history |
| Padre sin cabeza | Headless cleric and colonial religious landscape | Many localized versions; record place and collector |
| Tabudo | Lake or water-associated transformed figure | Check whether the story is localized to Lake Coatepeque and date of collection |
| Poza del Bululú | Enchanted pool, golden washing objects, Sensunapán/Sonsonate | A localized legend worth preserving even if late-recorded |
| Nahualismo and “brujos de Izalco” | Transformation narratives, specialists, stigmatization, and community identity | Colonial and modern outsiders may sensationalize living practice |
| Cangrejito de oro / enchanted treasure | Water, buried wealth, and sacred-place narratives | Often hybrid colonial and Indigenous motifs |

The best citation path begins with Carl Hartman's early Nahua/Pipil fieldwork
([1901 ethnographic study](sources/public-domain/hartman-1901-etnografiska-undersokningar-ofver-aztekerna-i-salvador.pdf)
and [1907 mythology article](sources/public-domain/jstor-534659.pdf)), then
Leonhard Schultze-Jena's 1930 Izalco recordings, and María de Baratta's later
folklore and ethnomusicological collection. The last two are linked in the
[source index](sources/INDEX.md) because the readily available editions require
more careful rights handling.

## Legendary and disputed historical figures

### Atlácatl

Treat Atlácatl as a mixed historical-memory problem. Pedro de Alvarado's letters
describe resistance and Salvadoran places but do not straightforwardly establish
the later national hero biography. Trace the name through early chronicles,
nineteenth-century histories, and twentieth-century state commemoration before
accepting any identification.

### Atonal

The warrior said to have wounded Alvarado is another high-value tradition whose
documentary chain must be reconstructed. Distinguish what Alvarado says about his
wound from later assignment of a personal name and biography.

### Antu Silan Ulap and Lenca royal traditions

These belong in the corpus as ethnohistorical/legendary traditions, with careful
attention to the date, community, and political context of their first surviving
record. Regional Lenca material from Honduras is relevant only where it helps
evaluate the Salvadoran tradition.

## Historiography and obsolete interpretations

Preserve these claims because they influenced research, but label them:

- direct Toltec conquest/colonization explanations for Cihuatán;
- artifact style equated automatically with ethnic migration;
- simple maps dividing El Salvador into bounded “Maya,” “Pipil,” and “Lenca”
  territories for all periods;
- the archaeological “frontier” treated as cultural absence rather than a zone
  of interaction;
- uncritical identification of Quelepa's entire sequence as Lenca;
- early racial and civilizational terminology in Montessus, Hartman, Squier,
  Bancroft, Lothrop, Spinden, and related works;
- nationalist invention or over-systematization of Indigenous pantheons;
- old dates for the Ilopango TBJ and Loma Caldera eruptions used without checking
  current research;
- “Pompeii of the Americas” as a useful publicity metaphor but a poor analytical
  model for Joya de Cerén.

The historiography itself is a subject. See [Escamilla and Fowler](sources/institutional/2012-escamilla-fowler-practica-arqueologia.pdf),
[Erquicia on the 1854 state antiquities inventory](sources/institutional/2014-erquicia-estadistica-1854-arqueologia.pdf),
[Montessus's 1891 study](sources/public-domain/BSG_DELTA700FA.pdf), and
[Lardé's 1924 survey](sources/public-domain/arqueologia-cuzcatleca-valle-de-san-salvador.pdf).

## Primary-source ladder

1. **1524 — Pedro de Alvarado's letters:** conquest route, battles, Cuscatlán,
   and the conqueror's own framing. Primary but self-justifying.
2. **Sixteenth-century Indigenous/auxiliary testimony and legal proceedings:**
   essential counterpoints where surviving editions can be located.
3. **1548 — Relación de Marroquín:** tribute, population, and early colonial
   transformations; a priority for deeper retrieval.
4. **1576 — Diego García de Palacio:** towns, languages, beliefs, production,
   geography, and ruins across San Salvador and Honduras.
5. **Seventeenth-century chronicles:** Remesal and Fuentes y Guzmán; later than
   conquest and frequently derivative, but crucial for the transmitted record.
6. **1797 — José Mariano Moziño on xiquilite/indigo:** production knowledge and
   colonial economy ([local facsimile](sources/fundar/anil_mozino.pdf)).
7. **1807 — Antonio Gutiérrez y Ulloa:** province-wide administrative description;
   linked for retrieval in the source index.
8. **1854 state statistics and 1859 municipal/Gaceta reports:** early state
   antiquities inventory and reports of ruins including the Cihuatán area.
9. **1855 — E. G. Squier:** direct observations and compiled geography of
   Honduras and San Salvador ([local copy](sources/public-domain/notesoncentralam00squi.pdf)).
10. **Late nineteenth/early twentieth century surveys:** Barberena, Montessus,
    Hartman, Peccorini, Spinden, Lardé, and early museum records.
11. **1930 onward Indigenous-language and folklore recordings:** Schultze-Jena,
    Baratta, and later community-centered language/oral-history work.
12. **Excavation archives:** Dimick, Boggs, Longyear, Andrews, Sharer, Sheets,
    Bruhns, Amaroli, Fowler, Salvadoran national projects, and university theses.

## Recommended first deep dives

1. **Reconstruct the Cuscatlán/Atlácatl/Atonal citation chain** from Alvarado
   through nineteenth- and twentieth-century national histories.
2. **Build a source-critical Izalco mythology concordance** across Hartman,
   Schultze-Jena, Baratta, modern Nawat scholarship, and community sources.
3. **Map every named site in the FUNDAR collection**, including rescue sites
   absent from Wikipedia.
4. **Reassess Cihuatán's “Mexican” connections** artifact by artifact instead of
   treating Toltec migration as a single explanation.
5. **Create an eastern El Salvador dossier** linking Quelepa, Corinto rock art,
   Cacaopera/Kakawira, Lenca attribution, Guatajiagua, and Gulf of Fonseca
   archaeology.
6. **Update the volcanic chronology** and tag every affected site by eruption,
   deposit, and confidence.
