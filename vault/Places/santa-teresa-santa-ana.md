# Finca Santa Teresa

## Description

An unexcavated monumental center recorded in a coffee finca southeast of Santa Ana. Stanley Boggs described a group of approximately ten mounds within the finca and a heavily damaged outlying mound along its approach road; Longyear's later summary reported about twelve mounds. The principal mound was approximately 19–21 m high and 45–60 m across at its base, making it one of the largest then known in El Salvador. Surface finds included numerous ceramic figurine fragments, carved stone heads, and a small stone human figure. Adobe construction was visible where the largest mound had eroded or been damaged. The site's occupation has not been dated directly. Its sculptures provide a probable association with the generally Late Preclassic Jaguar Head tradition, but that association is not a substitute for stratigraphic or ceramic dating. [[Papers/longyear#Page 29|Source: PDF p. 29 (printed p. 22), Santa Teresa entry]] [Source: Paredes Umaña, Boggs's 1943 notebook, PDF pp. 8–9 (printed pp. 238–239)](https://repositorio.ues.edu.sv/bitstreams/c16b8946-f26a-4bcc-984f-03248adf7acb/download#page=8)

## Location and identification

Boggs placed Finca Santa Teresa 8–10 aerial miles, or approximately 12–16 km, southeast of Santa Ana, on sloping terrain cut by streams along a natural passage between the Santa Ana and Zapotitán valleys. His party left the Pan-American Highway near kilometre 57 and followed a finca road for approximately 6 km. The landowner was Guillermo Battle of Santa Ana, and the main mound group lay about 0.5 km from the finca house. The published Longyear account gives a similar route but summarizes the distance as about 10 km southeast of Santa Ana. [[Papers/longyear#Page 29|Source: PDF p. 29 (printed p. 22), location, route, ownership, and site data]] [Source: Paredes Umaña, Boggs's 1943 notebook, PDF p. 8 (printed p. 238)](https://repositorio.ues.edu.sv/bitstreams/c16b8946-f26a-4bcc-984f-03248adf7acb/download#page=8)

The atlas marker is a locality proxy chosen because a mapped place named Finca Santa Teresa at approximately 13.911667, -89.461944 fits Boggs's distance and direction substantially better than the former marker on the southern edge of Santa Ana. Neither the archaeological mound footprint nor the historical finca boundary has been relocated on the ground. [Source: mapped Finca Santa Teresa locality](https://mapcarta.com/es/19813020)

### Distinction from nearby sites

The 1940s records support treating Finca Santa Teresa as separate from El Congo / El Bigote: the same survey describes El Congo independently as seven mounds extending west from the railroad station about 20 km southeast of Santa Ana, whereas Santa Teresa was a ten-to-twelve-mound group on Guillermo Battle's finca, reached by a different documented route. [[Papers/longyear#Page 27|Source: PDF pp. 27–28 (printed pp. 20–21), El Congo / El Bigote]] [[Papers/longyear#Page 29|Source: PDF p. 29 (printed p. 22), Santa Teresa]]

A 2020 secondary publication states that Santa Teresa, Finca Rosita, Finca Arizona, and Finca San José were names for a single settlement south of Santa Ana. Its cited source, however, distinguishes the Finca Rosita–Arizona–San José urban complex and refers to another southern property as **Santa Teresita**, not Boggs's Finca Santa Teresa. The 2020 statement therefore appears to conflate Santa Teresa with Santa Teresita and is not used here to merge the records. [Source: Szymański 2020, PDF p. 21](https://wuw.pl/data/include/cms/Estudios_Latinoamericanos_2020_40.pdf#page=21) [Source: Genovez 2013, discussion of the southern Santa Ana sites](https://repositorio.ues.edu.sv/bitstreams/e2c8d140-0016-4e51-aecf-a353dec1ace7/download)

## Architecture

Boggs could not prepare a reliable plan because coffee trees obscured the structures, but his notebook supplies approximate dimensions:

- The largest mound stood about 65–70 ft, or 19–21 m, high and measured roughly 150–200 ft, or 45–60 m, across its base. Boggs compared it with Mound 5 at San Andrés and Mound 1 at El Trapiche.
- Mounds 2 and 3 were elongated structures approximately 30–35 ft, or 9–10 m, high.
- Several low structures stood only about 3–5 ft, or 1–1.5 m, high and had become irregular through coffee cultivation.
- Damage near the northwest corner of the largest mound exposed adobe or adobe mortar. A damaged mound also stood north of the road about 1 km before the finca house.

[Source: Paredes Umaña, Boggs's 1943 notebook, PDF pp. 8–9 (printed pp. 238–239)](https://repositorio.ues.edu.sv/bitstreams/c16b8946-f26a-4bcc-984f-03248adf7acb/download#page=8)

## Sculptures and surface finds

Boggs photographed ceramic figurine fragments, carved stone heads, and a small carved human figure at the finca. One head reportedly came from the northwest corner of the largest mound and had a rear tenon, supporting its interpretation as an architectural ornament. Longyear described the mound group as unexcavated and the objects as surface finds or pieces reported from damaged contexts. [[Papers/longyear#Page 29|Source: PDF p. 29 (printed p. 22), surface finds and tenoned head]] [Source: Harvard Peabody Museum, Finca Santa Teresa photographic catalogue](https://collections.peabody.harvard.edu/search/%22Finca%20Santa%20Teresa%22/objects/table)

Later studies designate two Santa Teresa sculptures as Jaguar Head Monuments 12 and 17. Monument 12, the likely architectural example associated with the large mound, measures approximately 0.40 × 0.46 × 0.25 m. Monument 17, reported at the finca house, measures approximately 0.78 × 0.71 × 0.60 m. Paredes Umaña reported that both were measured and photographed in a private collection in Santa Ana in 2006; their present location has not been verified. [Source: Paredes Umaña 2018, Santa Teresa monument catalogue](https://es.scribd.com/document/913013452/Lo-Propio-y-Lo-Ajeno-Interculturalidad-y) [Source: Portillo Galán 2020, PDF p. 140](https://biblioteca.utec.edu.sv/siab/virtual/tesis/941001347.pdf#page=140)

The Harvard Peabody Museum catalogue preserves eight photographic records attributed to Finca Santa Teresa, including two views of the largest mound, ceramic figurine fragments, two carved heads, and the small stone human figure. These are photographic records rather than evidence that the physical objects entered the Harvard collection. [Largest mound, southwest corner](https://collections.peabody.harvard.edu/objects/392920/men-at-base-of-southwest-corner-of-largest-mound) [Largest mound, west side](https://collections.peabody.harvard.edu/objects/393064/west-side-of-largest-mound) [Ceramic figurine fragments](https://collections.peabody.harvard.edu/objects/432169/pottery-figurine-and-other-fragments) [Small carved stone human figure](https://collections.peabody.harvard.edu/objects/393077/small-carved-stone-human-figure-of-grey-igneous-stone)

## Chronology and interpretation

No controlled excavation, stratigraphic sequence, radiocarbon determination, or site-specific ceramic chronology has been published for Santa Teresa. The Jaguar Head sculptural tradition is generally assigned to the Preclassic, particularly the Late Preclassic, and regional analyses have modeled Santa Teresa as a smaller center within a hypothetical Chalchuapa-centered political landscape. Both conclusions are comparative interpretations rather than direct dating or proof of political subordination at Santa Teresa. [Source: Paredes Umaña, regional Jaguar Head analysis](https://www.asociaciontikal.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/Simp28-58-Paredes.pdf) [Source: Portillo Galán 2020](https://biblioteca.utec.edu.sv/siab/virtual/tesis/941001347.pdf)

## Studies

### 2020: Iconographic synthesis

Portillo Galán included Monuments 12 and 17 in a comparative iconographic study of the Jaguar Head tradition. The work synthesized earlier documentation and reported no new fieldwork at Santa Teresa. [Source: Portillo Galán 2020, PDF p. 140](https://biblioteca.utec.edu.sv/siab/virtual/tesis/941001347.pdf#page=140)

### 2011: Proposed archaeological project

A Department of Archaeology memorandum dated 21 March 2011 requested authorization for the "Proyecto Arqueológico Santa Teresa (Santa Ana) y Santa Marta (Sonsonate) 2011," directed by Federico Paredes Umaña. The document establishes that Santa Teresa was an intended research target. The thesis containing the memorandum instead describes the completed work as the San Jorge and Las Aradas project, and no Santa Teresa field report or results were located; authorization is therefore not treated as evidence that fieldwork occurred. [[Papers/finca-san-jorge-las-aradas#Page 258|Source: PDF p. 258 (printed p. 240), project-authorization memorandum]]

### 2006: Sculpture documentation

Paredes Umaña reportedly located, measured, and photographed Santa Teresa Monuments 12 and 17 in a private collection in Santa Ana. This documented displaced objects and did not constitute fieldwork at the archaeological site. [Source: Paredes Umaña 2018, Santa Teresa monument catalogue](https://es.scribd.com/document/913013452/Lo-Propio-y-Lo-Ajeno-Interculturalidad-y)

### Early 1943: Reconnaissance

Boggs visited the finca with landowner Guillermo Battle and representatives Baratta and Aguillón of the Museo Nacional. He recorded the route and setting, estimated the mound dimensions, observed damaged adobe construction, and photographed monuments and surface artifacts. Tree cover prevented a reliable count or plan, and no excavation was undertaken. His original notebook description was later summarized by Longyear and published in transcription and Spanish translation by Paredes Umaña. [[Papers/longyear#Page 29|Source: PDF p. 29 (printed p. 22), published survey summary]] [Source: Paredes Umaña, Boggs's 1943 notebook, PDF pp. 8–9 (printed pp. 238–239)](https://repositorio.ues.edu.sv/bitstreams/c16b8946-f26a-4bcc-984f-03248adf7acb/download#page=8)
