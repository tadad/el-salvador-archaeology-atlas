During one of my visits to San Salvador, capital of El Salvador, | came across a number of
mold-made ceramic figurine fragments of a type I had not encountered before in that
country. Originally one lot, said to have been found near Suchitoto, a city in the northern
part of the Departamento de Cuzcatlán, had been split up unevenly between two private
collectors. | was, however, able to see photograph, and investigate both parts

The total lot consisted of 24 fragments, of which twice two parts could be fitted
together, resulting in two more or less complete figures (table 1, n% 1 and 3). The resulting
22 units can be divided into three categories: three more or less complete figurines (table
1, n% 1-3), ten heads (n°* 4-13), and nine body fragments (n°* 14-22)

The clay of all picces is, as far as it could be ascertained, always heavily tempered. The
sand temper is rather coarse, with uneven grain sizes. The firing is uneven too, and resulted
often in incomplete oxydizing, leaving sometimes a grey (about 1OYR 7/2, light grey) core,
in spite of the relative thinness of the figurines. Obviously, a red-burning clay had been
used. The uneven firing resulted in different colors. Visually, the color of twelve fragments
was red, four could be called redbrown, while the remaining six were brown (sce table 1).
Munsell colors for six heads and two figures have been determined. They show a great
variety: only twice two figures or heads show the same color (n° 3 and 7: SYR 5/6,
yellowish red; n% 2 and 10: SYR 6/6, reddish yellow). It is interesting to note that, visually,
the color notations of these two pairs are reversed: n°* 2 and 7 were called “redbrown”, n* 3
and 10 “red”. In general, however, there is a certain clustering of colors in the 5/6-6/6-7/6
range, indicating that the same clay may have been used for all fragments (see also table 1).

Some of the fragments seem to be cither slipped or painted on the front side, especially
n°’ 11, 13, 17, and 20, the color being a rather light red (10YR 4/4, weak red). One of the
figures (n° 3) has a pink (7.S5YR 7/4) coating and one head (n° 6) and one body fragment
n° 19) have remnants of white (10YR 8/2) paint on it. Whether this indicates that the
fragments were all coated with a white base color and painted like many of the Mazapan

sures from Central Mexico (see f.i. Bernal et al. 1968, fig. 35) cannot be decided,

The Gross Type is especially present on faces, which are less raised and without a
well-defined chin. Eyes and mouth are indicated by raised outlines, shaped as simple pointed
ovals; the nose is triangular and the eyebrows, which begin at the root of the nose, only
slightly raised.

The mouth of the Fine Type is more raised, but again, like the eyes, rimmed by a thin
ridge. The corners of the mouth are more rounded and it is sometimes deepened at the
corners (figs. 4 and 6). Rarely, the upper lip is shaped by two curves mecting at the middle,
i.e. below the nose (figs. 7 and 10), giving the mouth a still more natural appearance. The
eyes are, again, pointed ovals, but usually with a slightly raised pupil in the middle.
Noses are longer than those of the Gross Type. Eyebrows, beginning again at the root of the
nose, are raised higher than those of the other type. Some of the “fine faces”, which all have
chins, show also slightly raised cheek bones, which never occur with the other type.

Both types show the same head ornaments. Surrounding the face on three sides are two
parallel raised ridges, horizontal above the face and vertical at the sides. The “corners” are
angular to rounded. The ridges may represent heavily stylized hair; therefore, they will be
hitherto called “hair-ridges”. In all cases, the vertical ones end at the earplugs. There are
round disks, usually with a raised center part. Above and beside the “hair-ridges” are two
flat elements, which meet above the middle of the head, leaving a more or less V-shaped
opening or notch in the crest. The elements end in a straight horizontal line more or less at
the same height as the “hair-ridges”, i.e. slightly above the carplugs. Whether these
elements, hitherto called (as in some other publications) “bilobed headdress” depict a
feather headdress is difficult to decide. I think, however, that this interpretation is correct.

These mold-made, flat, ginger-bread-like figurines —called galletas (crackers) in El
Salvador— are well-known in Mesoamerica. They are part of the Mazapan Phase or
Complex, the main archacological manifestation of the historic Toltecs and often dated
between A.D. 950 and A.D. 1050 (see f.i. Nicholson 1978, fig. 10.1).

These figurines are, however, rare in El Salvador. I had not seen similar figures in that
country before, neither during my extensive field surveys nor in the many private collections
I visited and, partly, photographed.

Among the published material from El Salvador, Bruhns mentions that a fragment of
“what may be a Mazapan figurine... was found in the excavation of SS-54” of Cihuatan, the
well-known Postclassic site in Central El Salvador (Bruhns 1980: 92-93). This may be either
the head or the body fragment of female Mazapan figurines mentioned by Fowler in his
thesis (Fowler 1981: 270). Unfortunately, the illustrations (fig. 594 and b) of these
fragments are (because I have only a photostat copy) so poor that details cannot be
distinguished. According to the description, the body fragment seems to be similar to those
in the collection under discussion. The only difference is that “the fingertips of the two
hands” are said to be “touching together” (Fowler 1981: 270), while the hands of the
Suchitoto figurines never touch. Since the photograph is so poor, this cannot be verificd.
The head, on the other hand, seems to be completely different, since it has a horizontal bar

headdress.

Besides this, Bruhns (1980: 93) mentions a complete Mazapan figure from Cihuatan in
the collections of the Museo Nacional David J. Guzmán of San Salvador, but does not
illustrate nor describe it. Finally, she states “that in 1975 or 1976 in the course of
clandestine excavations in a platform to the southwest of the Western Ceremonial Center [of
Cihuatan], a cache of some twelve of these figures” (i.e. Mazapan figurines), “all missing
heads, was found” (Bruhns 1980: 93). These cannot be the fragments discussed here, not

What about Central Mexico? Unfortunately, these Mazapan figures are not spectacular,
not aesthetically pleasing and, therefore, have often not been depicted in more general books
on Mesoamerica. As far as I was able to ascertain, no summary or investigation of all known
Mazapan figurines has been published since Linné first mentioned such a figure (Linné
1934: fig. 112) and coined the name. For Central Mexico, I am aware of only two larger
groups of Mazapan or Mazapan-like figures published from a single site: Stocker’s article
on the figures from Tula (1974) and Noguera’s group from Cholula (1954: 158-165).
Stocker mentions another study (Kaplan 1958), where Mazapan figures from Chiconauhtla
are discussed, but this is an unpublished master thesis and, therefore, was not available to
me for comparison,

Unfortunately, descriptions and photographs of the Cholula figurines are rather poor and
make comparison difficult. Also, I have not been able to find out the system by which
Noguera distinguished between his types. As for comparison, his Types 1 and 2, possibly
also Type 4, could include Mazapan figures, in spite of the fact that Noguera obviously
considers only Type | to be connected with that kind of figures (Noguera 1954: 161). Going
into details, bilobed headdresses, the most important and most casily detectable features are
shown on p. 154c (Type 1) and p. 155a (Type 2), the first one also possessing the double

There are two other Mexican regions where Mazapan or Mazapan-like figurines have
been found, obviously in larger numbers: Western Mexico and Veracruz. Therefore, these
regions have also to be considered as points of origin.

From Western Mexico, Vaillant already published in 1938 a figure which has some
similarities, especially through the presence of the bilobed headdress and the quechquemitl
(Vaillant 1938, fig. 2m). The best condensation of this type of figurines was published by
Grosscup in 1961, in connection with his excavations at Amapa, Nayarit. Of the 27 types
Grosscup established for his figurine sequence, 10 were mold-made. Of these, only 3
(Types A-C) I should consider to be either Mazapan or Mazapan-related. Most interesting
for us is Grosscup’s Type A (1961: 391-392, fig. 1A). Besides the usual flat appearance,
this type is characterized by “a two-lobed headdress, a circular pendant on the chest and a
V-shaped garment running from the shoulders to the waist. The hands may appear to grasp
the edge of this garment of lie parallel to it”. (Grosscup 1961: 391). Bilobed headdress and
the V-shaped garment, actually a quechquemitl, are also characteristics of our figurines,

while the circular pendant is missing. Certainly, the Amapa figures are much more

The Mazapan figures found at Cojumatlan, Michoacan, are again very similar to the
Amapa ones, but lack the circular pendant (Lister 1949: 59-62, figs. 25-26). They are,
therefore, more like those from El Salvador. On the other hand, they are again much more
elaborate and, like the Tizapan figurines, “are made of a fine tan paste” (Lister 1949: 59).
A figure fragment from Apatzingan, Michoacan, might also belong to this general
“West-Mexican” style (Kelly 1947, fig. 64D). Again similar to the Amapa type is the
fragment of a figurine which Winning (1987, tomo II, fig. IX-3h) recently published. It was,
allegedly, found in the Valley of Mexico. If that is true, it certainly must have been an
import.

Some more Mazapan figures have been reported from Western Mexico. They are,
however, cither too different from our type, like those from El Coralillo, Jalisco (Kelly
1949, fig. 81, pl. 25e-f) or too fragmentary like those from Ixtlan del Rio, Nayarit (Gifford
1950: 233, pl. 28) and Huistla, Jalisco (Glassow 1967: 81, fig. 7c). The latter two, however,
might well conform to the type. The mold-made head of an otherwise hollow, seated figure
from Nayarit (Noguera 1959, fig. 7) might also conform to our type. The body is, however,
too different, in spite of the fact that, allegedly, a quechquemitl is depicted (Noguera
1959: 502).

Summarizing Western Mexico, we can state that there are many similarities between one
type of Mazapan figures and those found in El Salvador. They are, however, not identical,
the West-Mexican ones being much more elaborate. Compared with them, the Salvadoran
objects look like poor cousins. One, therefore, cannot conclude that the Mazapan figurines
from Suchitoto have been imported from West-Mexico. Against that also stands the
difference in the material, i.e. the fine paste, obviously typical for the Mazapan figurines
from Western Mexico.

To summarize all said above, we can state that no identical Mazapan figures to those
from the Suchitoto region of El Salvador have been found. While the clay composition of
the Mazapan figurines from Central Mexico may be similar, the shapes and features,
especially the elaboration and ornamentation of the heads, are very different. On the other
hand, the significant bilobed headdress, probably a feather headdress, occurs in Western
Mexico and in Veracruz, as does the quechquemitl. But there are otherwise so many
differences too, that only a general similarity is present, perhaps originating in the
possibility that these figurines represent a specific goddess, perhaps a maize goddess. That
the Salvadoran figurines have been imported from either region is a possibility but it cannot
be proved. One may also consider the possibility that the figurines are actually made in El
Salvador, including their molds, but I doubt it very much, especially since this type of
figurines is so rare in that country. Therefore, the important question, from where these
Mazapan-like figurines came to El Salvador, has to remain open. The investigation also
proves the urgent need of a compendium for all known Mazapan figurines, including all
relevant details like clay composition, etc. to get a better understanding of the types and
distribution of this important and widely diffused figurine style.
