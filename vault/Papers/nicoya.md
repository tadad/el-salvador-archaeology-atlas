During an archaeological reconnaissance of the Costa del
Balsamo and its adjacent Cordillera a chance visit to an inland
farmer led to his producing a strange ceramic figurine which
had been found by his father while working one of his fields,
a piece of land called Portrero Coyol (alternatively, La Ceiba)
(Revene 2006: 12). The figurine is immediately identifiable as
being of the Nicoya tradition of Costa Rica and Nicaragua,
and pertaining to of the Papagayo/ Pataky type (figurines are
not classified into strict types) of ca. A.D. 1000 to 1300.
Hand-modeled, it is of oxidized fired clay, dark orange
in color (Munsell SYRS/S) and is approximately 16.S cm. in
height. The figurine represents a young woman sitting Japa­
nese style with her legs folded under her. As is common in
this style the folded legs are represented as hollow, closed,
tubes , without any division of the thigh and lower leg or
representation of the feet. The solid anns (one is broken off)
are akimbo and the figure looks straight ahead with an erect
head. The top of the head is indented in the middle and the
headdress is a raised headband . The heart-shaped face has the
typically very slanted, bugged out eyes of this style and small,
modeled ear spools adorn the lobes of the carefully modeled
ears. The hollow body of the figurine is modeled sensitively
with small breasts, indented waist, and gently swelling hips
and stomach. The female represented is definitely not preg­
nant. A large hole was cut into the back of the figurine to
assist in even firing.
The figurine is painted with a thick cream-white slip, now
somewhat eroded (MunseIl2.SYS/ I) with details of body paint
(?) and jewelry done in now eroded black slip. As is typical
of this style the eyebrows, eyes, and mouth were outlined in
black and the head above the modeled band was apparently
painted solid black. Although the black is nearly disappeared,
enough traces remain to show that the figurine originally was
painted with an elaborate "necklace" and "brassiere" in de­
signs which contained spiral elements. There also appears to
have been some black painting on the lower abdomen and in

the genital area, although this is almost entirely gone (Fig. I).
Very small and heavily eroded sections of red slip are found
in the inner recess of one ear, the midline of the lips , and the
hip on one side. No design can be determined based upon
such limited and eroded red slip, although its presence does
lend more evidence to possible origins .
Although Nicoya Polychrome vessels are commonly as­
sociated with Early Postclassic Guazapa Phase sites in El
Salvador, figurines of this tradition have not been reported
previously in El Salvador. Guazapa Phase figurines are either
local in inspiration or are of the widely spread Mazapan tradi­
tion, mould made and at, depicting females in quechquemetl
and long skirt (Bruhns and Amaroli 2006), whereas the local
style has mold-made heads and hand modeled bodies and
features mainly male figures Bruhns and Amaroli 2007)
seem to have been painted and certainly were not painted as
elaborately as this little lady (indeed, they mostly were not
painted at all). Moreover, although the Costa del Balsamo
is not at all well known archaeologically , Sr. Pedro Antonio
Ruiz B., on whose property the figurine was found, denied
that there were any other pre-Columbian remains in the vicin­
ity, a statement borne out by our survey of the area in which
the figurine had come to light when the elder Mr. Ruiz hit
it with his digging stick. Another interesting observation
about the site of this find is the fact that the degree of incline
is such that one would not expect a residential site to be on
the slope unless it had been terraced. The only other remains
reported, since the elder Mr. Ruiz dug round the area to see
if there was anything else to find , was a large fragment of an
olla, now lost. This might suggest that the figurine was some
sort of buried offering, placed in an olla, a kind of offering
not unknown in El Salvador. Unfortunately most offerings
have been found by looters, so we have little reliable infor­
mation concerning their form, differences, or distribution.
However, we do have a report from local inhabitants who
claim they found 12 Mazapan style figurines in a buried olla

What does this little lady mean, in terms of any under­
standing of cultural relationships in the Early Postclassic?
We know that this was a time of cultural turmoil in western
El Salvador and that there was heavy "Mexican" in uence
on local cultures. This was substantial enough to suggest that
western El Salvador was an active participant in international
trade networks and display systems of the time. The role of
cultures to the southeast of western El Salvador in the Salva­
doran interaction sphere is little known. Some of the Nicoya
pottery found in El Salvador may have been locally manufac­
tured, although Day and others have claimed that most Early
Postclassic Nicoya sty Ie trade wares (including those found in
a cash in Tula, Hidalgo) seem to have been made in the Rivas
area of Nicaragua, in other words, in the northern reaches of
the Nicoya Peninsula cultural area (Day 1994, 1997).
Mesoamerican traits such as ball courts, Mazapan figu­
rines, and Tohil Plumbate pottery are, essentially, missing in
Nicaragua and Costa Rica. The few pieces of metal found in
El Salvador, however, would seem to have had a Costa Rica
origin (based on their style, no physical analyses having been
undertaken). Likewise, routes of exchange are relatively un­
known, save that the eastern coast of Central America seems
to have been the main sea route, whereas the west coast, with
its dearth of good harbors and unpredictable and often violent
winds and storms, was inhospitable to long sea voyages, al­
though land trade up the coastal plain is a distinct possibility.
What this little figurine may suggest is that we should be look­
ing at a combination of short trip coastwise trade combined
with overland movements of goods, such s ceramics, metal
and so on. In the case of the Costa de Balsamo, the goods for
exchange were like those that are still trade goods today, bal­
sam for perfumes and medicine, perhaps cacao, cotton, indigo,
or the locally produced shellfish purple dye. The perishable
nature of many of the most important elements of national
and international trade leaves us with mainly conjecture, but
the chance find of this Nicoya figurine underlies the necessity
to look carefully at source area in considering the nature of
cultural interaction in prehispanic times.
