# San Isidro (sitio arqueológico)

## Discovery summary

San Isidro is a large Middle-to-Late Preclassic monumental site in the Department of Sonsonate. Szymański and Prejs report Proyecto Arqueológico San Isidro (PASI) excavations at Cerrito 1, the site's largest visible structure, and compare its evidence with Bolinas-type figurine finds elsewhere in south-eastern Mesoamerica. The reviewed article does not publish a reproducible site coordinate or boundary, so the interactive atlas uses the modern San Isidro locality only as a landmark proxy.

## Drone mapping and geophysics

PASI used drone photogrammetry—not airborne LiDAR—to map the settlement. An amateur drone located almost 40 mounds across roughly 4 km² in 2018. The 2021 campaign used a DJI Phantom 4 RTK and ground station to collect about 27,000 overlapping photographs across more than 6.5 km²; Agisoft Metashape and Pix4Dmapper generated a dense point cloud and digital elevation model. Visualization borrowed techniques used in a Guatemalan LiDAR project, but dense shade forest still produced missing or extrapolated areas, a limitation a laser survey might have reduced. The authors counted 51 visible structures as a minimum because modern settlement, cultivation, and forest obscured others. [Open-access article, PDF pp. 3–4](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jasrep.2022.103628)

In the presumed center, investigators combined 450 MHz GPR, cesium magnetometry, horizontal electrical-resistivity profiling, and vertical electrical sounding. GNSS-RTK positioned the GPR profiles to centimetre-scale precision. The surveys identified possible anthropogenic features as well as volcanic, agricultural, metal, and soil-moisture signatures; several linear or right-angled anomalies could not be assigned securely to ancient architecture. Targeted excavations at Cerrito and Trapiche then checked the remote-sensing interpretations and helped establish that some mounds were largely heaped or compacted clay without formal facings. [Open-access article, PDF pp. 5–10](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jasrep.2022.103628)

## Cerrito 1 tableau

During the 2022 season, excavation near the Cerrito 1 summit exposed five complete Bolinas-type clay figurines in a stratum dated by ceramic typology and radiocarbon assays to 410–380 BCE. They occupied just over 0.6 m² less than 0.5 m below the modern surface. Three figures (A–C) are approximately 0.30 m tall and made from fine cream-coloured paste; two (D–E) are approximately 0.18 and 0.10 m tall and made from coarser orange-brown paste. All retain white kaolinite slip, especially near the mouths and lower legs, and fainter red slip survives mostly on their backs. [PDF pp. 4–5 (printed pp. 783–784)](../vault/Papers/2025-szymanski-prejs-san-isidro-figurines.md#page-4)

The three large figures have adjustable heads. A conical neck projection fits a socket in the head, while drilled holes and channels allowed a string to pass through the neck and tie at the top. Figures B–E represent females; A represents a male and may be the first complete male Bolinas-type figure known. The male has an incised facial design interpreted cautiously as tattooing or scarification, along with a longer torso and narrower hips. The large figures have no modelled hair or jewellery, although holes in their earlobes may once have held detachable ornaments. D and E have modelled forelocks and earspools; E also has bracelets, a necklace or pectoral, and raised hands. [PDF pp. 5–6, 9 (printed pp. 784–785, 788)](../vault/Papers/2025-szymanski-prejs-san-isidro-figurines.md#page-5)

The authors reconstruct four figures as a west-facing row and the smallest as perhaps facing north. The two large females lay parallel to the mound axis; the small female D and large male A lay supine and parallel with their heads east; E lay prone with its head north. Four figures were broken, usually at the legs and neck. The associated offering included two polished jade disks, two stacks of serving vessels, and a smashed tripod metate. Daily removal of newly exposed objects and two large bullhorn-tree roots crossing the deposit could have disturbed the original arrangement, so the reconstruction is not treated as exact. [PDF pp. 5–7 (printed pp. 784–786)](../vault/Papers/2025-szymanski-prejs-san-isidro-figurines.md#page-5)

## Architecture and other finds

Cerrito 1 stands on the eastern edge of a large plaza opposite the smaller Trapiche 3 structure. In 2018, ploughing brought a plain stela to the surface of Trapiche 3. The authors project its original position near the structure's centre, aligned with Cerrito 1 and the site's principal axis about 15° north of west. If the figurines originally stood in the reconstructed row, they would have faced the stela and Trapiche 3 across the plaza. This architectural relationship is suggestive rather than proof of the tableau's performance or meaning. [PDF pp. 8–10 (printed pp. 787–789)](../vault/Papers/2025-szymanski-prejs-san-isidro-figurines.md#page-8)

Cerrito 1 fill contained many other figurine fragments, including a hollow upper body whose head and arms were broken away. Its smooth lower rim appears designed to sit on a detachable lower body, and tableau figure E fits inside its cavity. The authors raise birth re-enactment as one possible analogy but do not claim that the hollow body belonged to the tableau: about 4 m separated the two contexts. [PDF p. 12 (printed p. 791)](../vault/Papers/2025-szymanski-prejs-san-isidro-figurines.md#page-12)

The tableau lay in a stratum associated with enlargement of Cerrito 1. It may therefore have formed part of a termination rite for the older construction or a dedication for the successor. Vestiges of an earlier offering discovered in 2024 at the predecessor's floor level make a dedicatory sequence plausible, but the article does not establish the rite's purpose. [PDF p. 13 (printed p. 792)](../vault/Papers/2025-szymanski-prejs-san-isidro-figurines.md#page-13)

## Interpretation and limits

Szymański and Prejs propose that Bolinas figures may have functioned as puppet-like participants in static or moving enactments of stories or rites. At San Isidro, articulated heads, open mouths, empty earlobe holes, possible perishable dress, and kaolin around the mouths motivate questions about directional movement, speech or song, ritual feeding, and reusable roles. The evidence does not preserve the original performance, however. Intentional breakage, a cenotaph or vanished burial, a public rite, the identities represented, and birth imagery remain hypotheses. Only San Isidro and Tak’alik Ab’aj have secure primary-context Bolinas tableaux, an acknowledged sample too small for firm conclusions. [PDF pp. 7–12 (printed pp. 786–791)](../vault/Papers/2025-szymanski-prejs-san-isidro-figurines.md#page-7)

The authors interpret the summit deposit as probably connected to an important action of public character. Its restricted setting might have limited the immediate participants, while the plaza below could have held a larger audience. They argue that San Isidro may have maintained a relatively flat hierarchy capable of coordinated monumental construction, perhaps with leaders or a limited elite but without the extreme stratification seen farther west. This is a sociopolitical model, not a direct observation of governance. [PDF pp. 13–14 (printed pp. 792–793)](../vault/Papers/2025-szymanski-prejs-san-isidro-figurines.md#page-13)

## Regional connections and other Salvadoran localities

Bolinas-type figures occur at Middle and Late Preclassic sites along Pacific Guatemala and western El Salvador. The paper reports fragmentary examples from Chalchuapa, Santa Leticia, the Zapotitán Valley, and structural fill at Ataco. Most came from surface, fill, mixed, or secondary deposits. Finca Bolinas, about 6 km south of Chalchuapa, reportedly yielded 95 figures around the mid-twentieth century, but its discovery context is uncertain. Those localities are separately represented or cross-referenced in the interactive atlas without promoting secondary deposits into secure tableaus. [PDF pp. 2–3 (printed pp. 781–782)](../vault/Papers/2025-szymanski-prejs-san-isidro-figurines.md#page-2)

Formal and contextual parallels with Tak’alik Ab’aj suggest a shared ritual canon or community of practice, while avian celtiform pendants support longer interaction chains toward Nicaragua, Costa Rica, and Panama. The authors present San Isidro as directly connected to Mesoamerican networks while possibly sharing a less stratified political pattern with north-western Honduras. Similarity indicates interaction and shared technical or mental templates; it does not demonstrate identical rites, identities, or political institutions. [PDF pp. 11–14 (printed pp. 790–793)](../vault/Papers/2025-szymanski-prejs-san-isidro-figurines.md#page-11)
